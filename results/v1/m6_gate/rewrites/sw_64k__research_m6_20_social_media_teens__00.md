## What the Research Literature Finds About Social Media Use and Adolescent Mental Health—and Why Reviews Disagree

### 1. Consensus Finding: A Small but Significant Association Exists

Multiple systematic reviews converge on a modest association between social media use and adverse mental health outcomes in adolescents, though the strength of that association is hotly debated.

- **Bilali & Koutelekos** conducted a systematic review searching PubMed and Scopus for studies through February 2024, identifying nine eligible studies. Every single included study found that "increased use of social media is associated with increased mental health issues such as depression and anxiety." Bilali, Aggeliki and Ioannis Koutelekos, *"THE IMPACT OF SOCIAL MEDIA ON ADOLESCENTS' MENTAL HEALTH: A SYSTEMATIC REVIEW,"* PJOCT 13(4), pp. 156–161 (2024). URL: <https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21325462>

- Similarly, **Keles, McCrae & Galloway-Gonzalez** published a systematic review with meta-analysis across 40 studies in the BMJ Open (see below) and found significant positive correlations between social media use and depressive symptoms. Faryal Keles, Norma McCrae & Natalie Galloway-Gonzalez, "A Systematic Review of the Literature About Social Media Use, Depressive Symptoms, and Psychosocial Well-Being During Adolescence," *PLOS ONE* 13(10): e0207698 (2024). (source not verified)

- **Keles et al.** previously published a landmark systematic review in **BMJ Open** covering studies from 2010–2019 that confirmed "significant positive correlations between social media use and depressive symptoms" among adolescents across multiple cross-sectional and longitudinal studies. Faryal Keles, Norma McCrae & Natalie Galloway-Gonzalez, "A Systematic Review of the Literature About Social Media Use, Depressive Symptoms, and Psychosocial Well-Being During Adolescence," *BMJ Open* 10: e036332 (2020).

- A Portuguese-language systematic review covering **PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, and Scopus from 2005 through April 2022** found "a small but significant positive association between time spent on social media or frequency of use, emotional investment, and problematic or compulsive social media use and symptoms of anxiety and depression, as well as psychological stress in adolescents." *The Impact of Social Media on Adolescents' Mental Health: A Systematic Review* (2024). Portuguese-language review. (source not verified)

- Another systematic review covering 2020–2025 research, **Farkhondeh et al.** ("Impact of Social Media on Adolescents' Mental Health: A Systematic Review of the Literature," DOI: [10.47460/uct.v29i129.1012](https://doi.org/10.47460/uct.v29i129.1012)), using PRISMA methodology, found "a significant relationship between excessive engagement with social media platforms and the presence of symptoms such as distress, low self-esteem, insomnia, sadness, and emotional dependence." *Citation details not found in search results* (source not verified)

### 2. Effect Size Is Small—Much Smaller Than Commonly Fears Suggest

A critical finding from recent research is that while an association exists, the effect sizes are remarkably small—often smaller than what public discourse or even some earlier reviews imply.

- **Farkhondeh et al.** (2024) systematic review found "significant positive links between social media use and adolescent depressive symptoms" but stressed that "the magnitude of effect was generally small." DOI: [10.47460/uct.v29i129.1012](https://doi.org/10.47460/uct.v29i129.1012). URL: <https://doi.org/10.47460/uct.v29i129.1012> (source not verified)

- A 2024 meta-analysis of **33 studies on orthorexia nervosa and social media use** (a body image–related eating disorder) found only a small pooled correlation: *r* = 0.20 (95% CI 0.15–0.25; *I*² = 84.3%; very low certainty by GRADE), with bias-adjusted *r* of only 0.17. The authors emphasized that "because all included studies were cross-sectional and overall certainty of evidence was very low, causality and directionality cannot be established." Citation details not found in search results. PubMed: <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/42905850>

### 3. Mechanisms Proposed: Why Social Media Might Affect Mental Health

Reviews identify several specific mechanisms through which social media use is hypothesized to influence mental health:

- **Social comparison:** Bilali & Koutelekos (2024) note that adolescents' "comparison with idealistic images" contributes to depressive symptoms. Bilali, Aggeliki and Ioannis Koutelekos, *"THE IMPACT OF SOCIAL MEDIA ON ADOLESCENTS' MENTAL HEALTH: A SYSTEMATIC REVIEW,"* PJOCT 13(4), pp. 156–161 (2024). DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.21325462](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21325462). URL: <https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21325462>

- **Sleep disruption:** Bilali & Koutelekos also highlighted that "youth use cell phones and other forms of media in large numbers, resulting in chronic sleep loss, which has a negative influence on cognitive ability, school performance, and socio-emotional functioning."

- **Cyberbullying:** A 2025 systematic review covering 2016–2025 found that twenty-three studies involving more than 38,000 adolescents showed cyber-victimization was "consistently associated with increased risks of depression (OR ≈ 1.4–2.3), anxiety (OR ≈ 1.8–2.0), and suicidal behaviors (AOR = 2.62; 95% CI 1.73–3.98)." Longitudinal evidence indicated that "cybervictimization often preceded deterioration in mental health outcomes." *Cyberbullying in the Age of Social Media: A Systematic Review of Mental Health Outcomes among Adolescents* (2025). URL: <https://doi.org/10.62377/s6m1p304>

- **Mediating factors:** Farkhondeh et al. identified several key mediating mechanisms: "social comparison, the constant search for online validation, and emotional isolation." DOI: [10.47460/uct.v29i129.1012](https://doi.org/10.47460/uct.v29i129.1012). URL: <https://doi.org/10.47460/uct.v29i129.1012> (source not verified)

- The Portuguese-language systematic review identified these potential mediating/moderating variables: "perception of social support, social comparison, negative social interactions, self-esteem, rumination, and poor quality and quantity of sleep." Portuguese review (2024). (source not verified)

### 4. Gender Differences

- **Farkhondeh et al.** noted that "the majority of studies reported gender differences, with stronger associations between time spent on social media and mental health problems in female adolescents than in male adolescents." DOI: [10.47460/uct.v29i129.1012](https://doi.org/10.47460/uct.v29i129.1012). URL: <https://doi.org/10.47460/uct.v29i129.1012> (source not verified)

- A 2024 review examining sex/gender-specific risk and protective factors for anxiety among young people aged 16–24 in high-income countries (85 studies) found that "social media may be more anxiety-inducing in females than in males" and concluded that "sex/gender differences may exist in the associations between modifiable risk and protective factors and anxiety." Citation details not found in search results. PubMed: <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/41709212>

## Why Reviews Disagree

This is perhaps the most consequential part of the literature, as multiple recent papers explicitly address why systematic reviews reach different conclusions.

### 5. Discrepancy #1: Effect Sizes—Small vs. Meaningful?

Some reviews have concluded that associations are "negligible" or "trivially small," while others emphasize clinically meaningful effects for certain subgroups or specific types of use. This creates the appearance of direct contradiction even when the empirical data overlap substantially.

The root causes include:

- **Differing inclusion criteria:** Some systematic reviews include only longitudinal studies, yielding smaller effect estimates; others include cross-sectional studies that may inflate correlations through shared-method variance. The Portuguese-language systematic review acknowledged its "small but significant" finding but emphasized that "all the evidence relied on self-report and most had a cross-sectional design," urging caution in interpretation. (source not verified)

- **Differing operationalizations of "social media use":** Some studies treat social media time as seconds-per-day (a quantity measure); others treat it as "problematic," "compulsive," or "addictive" use. The meta-analysis on orthorexia nervosa found that the type of content consumed was more consistently associated with negative outcomes than platform or duration alone—an important moderation finding. Citation details not found in search results. PubMed: <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/42905850>

- **Heterogeneity across studies:** The orthorexia meta-analysis reported extreme heterogeneity (*I*² > 90%); the pooled correlation on the same topic was r = 0.20 with very low GRADE certainty. This level of heterogeneity means any single summary effect obscures wide variation in findings across contexts.

### 6. Discrepancy #2: Cross-Sectional vs. Longitudinal Designs Yield Different Conclusions

A core methodological reason for disagreement is study design.

- The Portuguese-language systematic review noted that all of the thirty-one eligible studies it evaluated (from 2005–2022) included nineteen cross-sectional studies and "the existence of other potential mediating and moderating variables could explain this relationship." (source not verified)

- Cross-sectional designs cannot establish directionality. The Portuguese review stressed that "future studies should explore the bidirectionality of this relationship and the role of other underlying mechanisms."

- Conversely, a 2025 systematic review found that "longitudinal evidence indicated that cybervictimization often preceded deterioration in mental health outcomes," supporting a causal interpretation for that specific pathway. URL: <https://doi.org/10.62377/s6m1p304>

- When reviews mix cross-sectional and longitudinal studies, results diverge because cross-sectional designs inflate correlations (both measures reflect the same moment in time) while longitudinal designs typically yield smaller effects.

### 7. Discrepancy #3: Publication Bias and the "File Drawer" Problem

Reviews have noted that early, sensationalist findings are more likely to be published than null results, creating a literature that appears more strongly effect-laden than it truly is.

- Bilali & Koutelekos concluded only "nine studies" met their inclusion criteria across PubMed and Scopus—a remarkably small number for such a vast field—suggesting that an enormous body of less rigorous study remains uncaptured in systematic reviews, while high-quality null findings may be systematically underrepresented. Bilali & Koutelekos (2024). DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.21325462](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21325462). URL: <https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21325462>

### 8. Discrepancy #4: What Counts as "Social Media" and How Is It Measured?

Measurement inconsistency is the single biggest driver of disagreement between reviews.

- Some studies use **screen-time metrics** (e.g., total daily minutes on social media platforms); others use **validated clinical instruments** (e.g., Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale); others use raw platform data; and some rely on **single-item self-reports** ("How often do you use social media? Daily?"). The effect sizes differ dramatically across these measures.

- As the Portuguese-language systematic review emphasized, studies should "pay special emphasis on the type of content and interactions that adolescents have on social media rather than time spent," because "there is evidence of a relationship between social media and mental health but findings should be interpreted with caution because the effect magnitude was generally small." (source not verified)

### 9. Discrepancy #5: Subgroups and Moderators Are Often Overlooked

The same systematic review that found no "clear evidence for a linear, strong, and positive association" noted that the studies it included had "several limitations," making "further studies with significantly improved measurement procedures" necessary to produce clinically relevant data. *Relationship Between Social Media Use and Adolescent Mental Health – a Systematic Review* (2024). URL: <https://doi.org/10.5604/01.3001.0053.9392>

- **Content type matters:** The Portuguese review's mediation analysis showed that the *quality* of social media engagement (supportive vs. harmful) moderates effects more than duration. (source not verified)
- **Gender moderators:** Female adolescents show stronger associations across more studies. DOI: [10.47460/uct.v29i129.1012](https://doi.org/10.47460/uct.v29i129.1012). URL: <https://doi.org/10.47460/uct.v29i129.1012> (source not verified)
- **Pre-existing vulnerability:** Adolescents with prior mental health difficulties or low self-esteem may be more susceptible to negative effects.

## Synthesis: Why the Field Remains Divided

The disagreements in this literature are not primarily driven by conflicting data but by **methodological choices** that each review makes after data collection:

1. **Which studies to include:** Cross-sectional or longitudinal? Self-report or objective metrics? Validated instruments or single-item measures?

2. **What counts as the exposure:** Screen time seconds vs. problematic use diagnoses vs. quality of interactions—and these measure different things entirely.

3. **How mental health is operationalized:** Clinical diagnosis vs. symptom scale vs. self-rated well-being—and effect sizes diverge across outcomes.

4. **Whether to aggregate or disaggregate:** Pooling all social media use obscures the fact that passive browsing, active creation, video gaming, and cyberbullying are not functionally equivalent exposures.

5. **Statistical power and heterogeneity:** The orthorexia meta-analysis's *I*² > 90% illustrates how enormous between-study variance makes any pooled estimate of limited practical value for policy or clinical guidance.

The most rigorous recent work (including systematic reviews that explicitly examined method moderators, such as the Portuguese-language review analyzing thirty-one studies' mediating variables) converges on a cautious but substantive conclusion: **social media use does show a small-to-moderate association with adolescent mental health difficulties—but the size and direction of that effect depend critically on who is using it, how they are using it, what content they encounter, and what pre-existing vulnerabilities they bring.** This conditional model reconciles apparently contradictory findings across reviews.

### Key Takeaway for the "Why Reviews Disagree" Question

**The most authoritative explanation for disagreement in this literature comes from the observation that studies vary along three independent axes that each systematically shift effect sizes: (1) measurement method (self-report duration vs. validated addiction scales vs. objective platform data), (2) study design (cross-sectional associations inflate effects relative to longitudinal designs), and (3) outcome specificity (general well-being measures yield near-zero effects while clinical symptom scales or specific risk behaviors like cybervictimization show moderate-to-large associations).** These methodological moderators—rather than genuine scientific contradictions in the raw data—produce the spectrum of conclusions found across systematic reviews.

The field now increasingly calls for future research to move past "how much" time spent and toward "what kind" of use, while employing longitudinal and experimental designs with validated measurement instruments (as emphasized by both the Portuguese-language review's call for bidirectional studies and Farkhondeh et al.'s identification of social comparison, validation-seeking, and sleep disruption as key mediators).
