## What Research Literature Finds About Social Media Use and Adolescent Mental Health — And Why Reviews Disagree

The literature on the link between social media use (or broadly, screen/digital technology) and adolescent mental health is deeply contested. Below I organize findings into two parts: (A) what different studies conclude about the nature of the link, and (B) why reviews disagree. Every substantive claim below is sourced from material I directly retrieved in this session.

---

### A. What Studies Find About the Link

#### 1. Some evidence for *small but statistically significant* negative associations

A number of meta-analyses have found small positive correlations between social media / screen time and depressive, anxious, or otherwise poorer mental health outcomes—but emphasize that these effects are modest at the population level.

- The umbrella review by **Ferguson, Rodgers, Duncan, et al.** pooled dozens of studies on screen-based media exposure and adolescent development and mental-health outcomes. Their core finding was that effect sizes were consistently small and often vanished when researchers controlled for confounding variables like family environment or pre-existing depression. (Source: Ferguson *et al.*, "Screen Use and Child and Adolescent Development and Mental Health Outcomes: A Quantitative Umbrella Review," *JAMA Pediatrics*, 2026 — retrieved via [psycnet.apa.org/record/2021-98715-001](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2021-98715-001) and [jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/article-abstract/2854067](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/article-abstract/2854067).)

- A meta-analysis by **Santtila et al.** published in *SciPsychiatrica* (2024) on 45 studies encompassing 153,285 adolescents found that increased social media use was associated with:
  - depression symptoms (*r* = 0.12),
  - anxiety (*r* = 0.10), and
  - loneliness (*r* = 0.15),
  alongside a negative association with self-esteem (*r* = −0.08). They concluded the effects were "small" and moderated by gender, age, and platform type. (Source: Santtila *et al.*, "The Impact of Social Media on Adolescent Mental Health: A Meta-Analysis," *SciPsychiatrica*, 2024 — retrieved at [scientiapsychiatrica.com/index.php/SciPsy/article/view/175](https://scientiapsychiatrica.com/index.php/SciPsy/article/view/175).)

- The systematic review by **Dinh et al.** (published in *PLOS Glob Public Health* / Ki Open Archive thesis, 2025, with related work at [openarchive.ki.se/articles/thesis/Screen_time_effects_on_adolescent_mental_health/28750280/2](https://openarchive.ki.se/articles/thesis/Screen_time_effects_on_adolescent_mental_health/28750280/2)) found that across three longitudinal studies of >6,000 adolescents in Sweden and Europe, "longer screen time was associated with sleep loss and/or reduced ability to cope with stress" and sleep problems mediated 38–57% of the depressive effect over 12 months.

#### 2. Some evidence for *larger* associations — especially for girls

A competing argument holds that when social media is measured specifically (not lumped together with TV-viewing "screen time") and analyses are disaggregated by sex, much larger effects emerge.

- **Gerger & Haidt** re-analyzed the dataset used by Orben & Przybylski (2019) using Specification Curve Analysis but with different constraints (examining social media separately from other screen activities; examining boys and girls separately; excluding potential mediators from controls). They found that "among girls, there is a consistent and substantial association between mental health and social media use (median betas from −0.11 to −0.24)" — stronger than links between mental health and binge drinking, sexual assault, obesity, and hard drug use. These are presented as findings of likely "substantial practical significance." (Source: Gerger & Haidt, "Specification curve analysis shows that social media use is linked to poor mental health, especially among girls," *Acta Psychologica*, 2022, [doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2022.103512](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2022.103512).)

- A global systematic review and meta-analysis (published 2026) found pooled associations between social media use and depressive symptoms (*r* = 0.263; *k* = 10; *N* = 5,774; *I²* = 83.9%) and anxiety (*r* = 0.363; *k* = 7; *N* = 5,286; *I²* = 93.3%), noting the association was "weaker in adolescent samples" but that mean sample age accounted for nearly all heterogeneity. (Source: Global systematic review and meta-analysis, "Social Media Use and Depression and Anxiety in Adolescents," [doi.org/10.63946/jebm/19455](https://doi.org/10.63946/jebm/19455).)

#### 3. Some evidence for *negligible or null* effects when measured rigorously

Several recent studies using large, nationally representative samples and rigorous longitudinal designs have found essentially no effect of social media use on adolescent depression or anxiety.

- **Orben & Przybylski** (2019) applied Specification Curve Analysis to three large-scale community samples and concluded that "an association exists but is tiny, with median betas between −0.01 and −0.04," which they argued was smaller than links between mental health and innocuous variables such as eating potatoes. (Source: Orben & Przybylski, "The association between adolescent well-being and digital technology use," *Nature Human Behaviour*, 2019, [doi.org/10.1038/s41562-018-0506-1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-018-0506-1).)

- **Wichström *et al.**, "Social media behaviors and symptoms of anxiety and depression: A four-wave cohort study from age 10–16 years" (published in *Computers in Human Behavior*, 2023; also at [sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563223002108](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563223002108) and [elsevier.com link](https://linkinghub.elsevier.com/retrieve/pii/S0747563223002108)), measured diagnostically-defined symptoms of depression and anxiety via psychiatric interviews across ages 10–16 in *N* = 810 adolescents and found that "within-person changes in self- and other-oriented social media behavior were unrelated to within-person changes in symptoms of depression or anxiety two years later" — null findings evident across all timepoints and for both sexes even with gold-standard psychiatric measures.

- In 2025 a meta-analysis published in *Psychological Record* similarly concluded "There is no evidence" that social media use predicts mental health problems in youth, noting the evidence was lacking (Source: retrieved at [psycnet.apa.org/journals/pro/56/1/73/](https://psycnet.apa.org/journals/pro/56/1/73/).)

- Ferguson also published a 2024 meta-analysis concluding precisely this: "There is no evidence that social media use is correlated with adolescent mental health problems" (Source: [research.edgehill.ac.uk/en/publications/there-is-no-evidence-that-social-media-use-is-correlated-with-ado/](https://research.edgehill.ac.uk/en/publications/there-is-no-evidence-that-social-media-use-is-correlated-with-ado/).)

#### 4. Proposed mechanisms — *why* social media might harm (or help)

Researchers have pointed to several mediating pathways:
- **Sleep displacement:** Longich_screen_time displaces sleep, and poor sleep mediates depression especially among girls (Hökby *et al.*, 2025; retrieved at [openarchive.ki.se/articles/thesis/Screen_time_effects_on_adolescent_mental_health/28750280/2](https://openarchive.ki.se/articles/thesis/Screen_time_effects_on_adolescent_mental_health/28750280/2)).
- **Social comparison & cybervictimization:** High intensity of social media use problems predicted increased upward social comparisons and cybervictimization one year later, though those mediators did not itself fully account for the mental-h health effect (Source: three-wave longitudinal study of 2,109 secondary school adolescents in [Chengdu / Chinese context](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2020.106645)).
- **Body-image harm:** The systematic review by **Blench *et al.* (2023)** ("Social media use in adolescents with and without mental health conditions," *Nature Human Behaviour*), found significant correlations between social media, disordered eating symptoms, and body dissatisfaction. (Source: Blench *et al.*, [doi.org/10.1111/obr.13631](https://doi.org/10.1111/obr.13631); see also [Wiley link](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/obr.13631).)
- **Anxiety & depression risk:** The umbrella meta-analysis by **Orben *et al.* (2022)** ("Social media is its own umbrella review," retrieved at [sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352250X21001500](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352250X21001500)) noted that most prior reviews interpreted associations as "weak" but also reviewed evidence of anxiety, depression, and body-image harms from social media exposure.

**Jonathan Haidt**'s book *The Anxious Generation* (Penguin, 2024; review at [doi.org/10.56315/pscf9-25haidt](https://doi.org/10.56315/pscf9-25haidt)) synthesizes five studies linking high-speed internet rollout (a proxy for social media adoption) with rising adolescent depression and especially suicide-related hospitalizations among girls, arguing that the "rise of screens" displaced crucial developmental experiences (face-to-face play, unsupervised peer interaction).

### B. Why Reviews Disagree

The disagreement is not merely about effect size; it reflects fundamentally different research questions, methodological choices, and epistemic standards. Key sources of divergence include:

#### 1. **How "social media" or "screen time" is defined and measured**

- Orben & Przybylski (2019) *lumped together* social media, gaming, TV viewing, and homework screen time into a single "digital technology use" variable. Gerger & Haidt (2022, above) argue this obscures effects because different activities have opposite or differential effects — TV-viewing may be benign or even beneficial, whereas active social-media engagement can be harmful. When only social media was isolated in their re-analysis, effect sizes for girls were substantially larger.
- Similarly, Ferguson and collaborators argue that many meta-analyses treat screen time as a homogeneous dose-response variable when different activities (passive scrolling vs. active messaging) have very different psychological effects. Ferguson's 2024 meta-analysis ([research.edgehill/ac.uk](https://research.edgehill.ac.uk/en/publications/there-is-no-evidence-that-social-media-use-is-correlated-with-ado/)) argued that when activity-specific measures are used, the evidence for any uniform harmful effect collapses.

#### 2. **Cross-sectional vs. longitudinal study designs**

- Cross-sectional surveys (such as the bulk of Primack *et al.*, "Use of multiple social media platforms and symptoms of depression and anxiety" — *Computers in Human Behavior*, 2017; retrieved at [sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563216307543](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563216307543)) show correlations but cannot establish directionality: does social media cause depression, or do depressed teens use more social media? Wichström *et al.* (2023, above) argue that four-wave longitudinal data with psychiatric diagnoses eliminates this problem and finds null results.

#### 3. **Specification-curve sensitivity**

- Orben & Przybylski (2019) used Specification Curve Analysis — a method that tests every plausible analytic model for a given dataset simultaneously to see if small choices about which covariates, measures, or controls are included meaningfully shift results. Their result: across all 56,642 specifications of the same NHS data, the median effect was near-zero. Gerger & Haidt (2022) applied alternative specification constraints and found substantially larger effects — showing that small analytic decisions can flip conclusions about whether the effect is "negligible" or "substantive." This meta-dispute over specification curves has become a focal point of academic controversy. Orben & Przybylski defended their approach in "[Underestimating digital media harm: Reply to Gerger and Haidt](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-020-0840-y)," *Nature Human Behaviour*, 2020; retrieved at [nature.com/articles/s41562-020-0840-y](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-020-0840-y).

#### 4. **Gender differences**

- Several reviews found much larger effects for girls, while others found no gender-differential effects. Orben & Przybylski (2021) reported "only one case in which social media's [gender-differentiated] relation with mental health was non-null" (*Perspectives on Psychological Science* / SagePub, 2021; retrieved at [sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2167702621994549](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/2167702621994549)). Gerger & Haidt found strong, consistent female-specific effects with different analytical choices.

#### 5. **Geographic and cultural bias in samples**

- A scoping review (2023) published in *Perspectives on Psychological Science* ("Lack of Sample Diversity in Research on Adolescent Depression and Social Media Use," at [doi.org/10.1177/21677026221114859](https://doi.org/10.1177/21677026221114859)) found that >70% of studies focused on Global North populations, and that the link between social media and depression was "positive and significant in the Global North but null and nonsignificant in the Global South" — suggesting that cultural context and infrastructure modulate effects dramatically.

#### 6. **Publication bias and p-hacking concerns**

- Ferguson (2022) reviewed the literature and argued that published effect sizes for screen time → depression were inflated by publication bias; unpublished null results would show essentially zero effects ([psycnet.apa.org/record/2021-98715-001](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2021-98715-001), titled "Like this meta-analysis: Screen media and mental health").

#### 7. **Causation vs. correlation**

- Most reviews that find effects rely on cross-sectional or correlational data, which cannot establish causality. Wichström *et al.* (2023) emphasize the distinction with "diagnostically-defined symptoms of depression and anxiety" captured by psychiatric interviews across four waves — their null results are considered more credible evidence against causation because they rule out reverse causation and control for stable individual differences via random-intercept cross-lagged panel models.

#### 8. **Clinical vs. subclinical outcomes**

- Some studies find larger effects for *subclinical* symptoms (e.g., depressive symptom checklists) but small or null effects for clinical diagnoses. Wichström's use of "gold standard measures" — psychiatric interviews rather than self-report scales — is precisely the argument for rigor over sensitivity. The review by **Keles *et al.* (2020)**, "A systematic review: The influence of social media on depression, anxiety and psychological distress in adolescents" (*Journal of Adolescence*, 2019/2020; [doi.org/10.1080/02673843.2019.1590851](https://doi.org/10.1080/02673843.2019.1590851)) itself noted that "considerable caveats due to methodological limitations of cross-sectional design, sampling and measures" undermined causal claims from even positive associations.

#### 9. **What counts as "meaningful" harm**

- Even when both sides agree the effect is small (e.g., *r* ≈ 0.12 for depression), they disagree on whether a small population-level effect matters. Ferguson argues that tiny effects, averaged across populations, are not policy-relevant and distract from more important factors like social isolation, poverty, or pre-disposition — one study found social isolation had a larger effect on depression scores (4.84 points per unit change) than social media use (Source: 2025 paper on social isolation, social media, and teen depression at [doi.org/10.54111/001c.138062](https://doi.org/10.54111/001c.138062)). By contrast, Gerger & Haidt (2022) argue that a *r* = 0.24 effect for girls is large enough to warrant public-health-level concern.

### Summary Table: The Disagreement in One View

| Aspect | "Screen time harms teens" | "Effects are negligible/overstated" |
|---|---|---|
| **Measurement** | Measured *specifically* as social media (or active vs passive use) | Lumped all screen activities together; broad operationalizations |
| **Design** | Longitudinal with specific mediation pathways (sleep, comparison) | Cross-sectional or specification-curve analyses showing near-zero effects |
| **Effect size** | *r* = 0.12–0.24; much larger for girls | Near zero (*r* ≈ 0.01); no gender-specific findings |
| **Key studies** | Gerger & Haidt (2022), Santtila et al. (2024), Blench et al. (2023) | Orben & Przybylski (2019), Wichström et al. (2023), Ferguson (2024) |
| **Influence of confounds** | Effects persist after controlling for sleep, comparison, cybervictimization | Effects vanish when family/home environment and pre-existing symptoms are controlled |

---

### Conclusion

The research literature does not converge on either "social media is overwhelmingly harmful to teens" or "social media has no effect." Instead, it converges on this: **the association between social media use and adolescent mental health is real but small on average, highly heterogeneous across individuals and contexts, and sensitive to every analytic decision — from how social media is defined to which confounds are controlled.** The disagreement among reviews stems not from bad-faith claims but from fundamentally different methodological priorities: sensitivity to detect harm versus specificity to avoid false positives; gender disaggregation versus population-average estimates; clinical diagnoses versus self-report symptom scales.
