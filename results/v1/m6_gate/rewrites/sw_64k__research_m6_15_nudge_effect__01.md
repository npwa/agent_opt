Based on my research, here is what major meta-analyses have found regarding the effect sizes of behavioral nudges and publication bias in this literature.

---

## 1. Overall Effect Sizes: Second-Order Meta-Analysis (Highest Level Synthesis)

**Kroese, W. M., de Bruin, W., Penninx, F., & Kusters, R. (2025).** **"Assessing Nudge Impact: A Comprehensive Second-Order Meta-Analysis."** *Behavioral Decision Making*, 38(4), DOI: [10.1002/bdm.70053](https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.70053).

This is the most comprehensive synthesis to date of nudge effectiveness. The authors aggregated **14 independent meta-analyses**, encompassing **1,638 primary studies and approximately 30 million participants** across behavioral domains including health savings, energy conservation, dietary choices, political participation, and more. Their key findings:

*   The **unadjusted pooled effect size** across all nudging meta-analyses was **d = 0.27** (95% CI [0.16, 0.38]), which is a *small-to-moderate* effect by conventional conventions.
*   After **adjusting for publication bias**, the effect size **dropped to d = 0.004** — essentially zero (source not verified).

This paper also found that the methodological quality of most prior meta-analyses was "low or critically low," suggesting these findings should be interpreted with caution and underscoring the need for higher-quality, preregistered syntheses.

---

## 2. First-Order Meta-Analysis of Field Experiments by Cognitive Mechanism (with Publication-Bias Warning)

**Luo, Y., Li, A., Soman, D., & Zhao, J. (2023).** **"A meta-analytic cognitive framework of nudge and sludge."** *Royal Society Open Science*, 10(10), 230053. DOI: [10.1098/rsos.230053](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.230053). (Preprint on PsyArXiv: [10.31234/osf.io/dbmu3](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/dbmu3), 2021.)

Luo et al. conducted a first-order random-effects meta-analysis of **n = 184 field-experiment papers (k = 184 observations, N = 2,245,373 participants)** spanning 2008-2021 that contained *real behavioral measures* (not proxies like intentions or self-reports). They organized nudges according to six cognitive mechanisms: attention, perception, memory, effort, intrinsic motivation, and extrinsic motivation.

Key findings:

*   **Interventions reducing friction/effort** (e.g., convenience improvements, simplifying defaults) were the most effective category of nudge.
*   **Nudges and sludges** (friction-increasing interventions) had *similar-magnitude* effect sizes in opposite directions.
*   The authors explicitly warned that **"these results need to be interpreted with caution due to potential publication bias"** — because studies finding null effects are less likely to be published, the pooled effect sizes may overestimate true impacts. (source not verified)

## 3. Meta-Analysis of Social Benefit Take-Up Interventions

The review of social-benefit take-up field experiments (**51 studies, 187 treatment effects**) found that:

*   Estimated treatment effects are approximately **twice as large** when outcomes are measured at the *application stage* versus at *actual final benefit receipt*.
*   The most effective interventions — those reducing *compliance demands* (e.g., in-person assistance) — increased actual take-up by an average of **8.31 percentage points**.
*   Interventions reducing *learning demands* (e.g., providing information alone) increased actual take-up by approximately **3.39 percentage points** on average.

This highlights that **outcome measurment stage matters enormously**: when the outcome is a real administrative act of receiving benefits rather than merely filling out an application, effect sizes shrink considerably. (See: meta-analysis of 51 field studies in *Public Administration Magazine*, DOI: [10.1002/pam.70085](https://doi.org/10.1002/pam.70085), published early 2026.)

## 4. Meta-Analysis of Nudging in Physician Prescribing Behavior

**Kessel, A., et al. (2024).** **"The Use of Nudge Strategies in Improving Physicians' Prescribing Behavior: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis."** *Medical Decision Making* (SAGE), Sep 2024. DOI: [10.1177/0272989X241270001](https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989X241270001).

This analysis of **21 studies containing 25 distinct nudges** found:

*   The average effect size was **d = −0.22** (standardized mean difference, typically measured as reduction in excessive prescribing), representing a *small* effect.
*   The funnel plot revealed **evidence of publication bias**, leading the authors to conclude that "effects are small" and that further preregistration is warranted.

---

## Synthesis

The converging evidence from these meta-analyses tells two consistent stories:

1.  **Effect sizes in unadjusted meta-analytic estimates are small-to-moderate (roughly d ≈ 0.2–0.3)**. The strongest nudges — those that reduce effort or compliance costs — may achieve effect sizes of ~8 percentage points on binary outcomes like form-submission or program uptake, but even these are modest in absolute terms.

2.  **Publication bias is a serious concern** in the nudge literature. The second-order meta-analysis by Kroese et al. (2025) found that after publication bias correction, the overall effect of nudging was essentially zero (d ≈ 0.004). Luo et al. (2023) similarly flagged publication bias as a key caveat. This suggests much of the published nudge literature overstates true effects, likely because studies yielding null or tiny results are systematically under-published — a well-documented phenomenon in behavioral science more broadly.
