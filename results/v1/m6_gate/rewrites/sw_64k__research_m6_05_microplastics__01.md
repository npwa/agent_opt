# Microplastics in Human Tissue and Their Health Effects: A Synthesis of Current Evidence

What follows is drawn from published systematic/scoping reviews, experimental studies, and the landmark prospective cohort study referenced across multiple 2024–2026 narrative reviews. Every factual claim below is tied to a specific retrieved source; where no DOI or URL could be obtained, the PubMed ID is supplied.

---

## 1. What microplastics have been detected in human tissues and fluids?

### Widespread detection across organ systems
A scoping review of 26 in-vivo human studies, screening 3,616 articles under PRISMA guidance, found **microplastics detected in 8 out of 12 human organ systems**: cardiovascular, digestive, endocrine, integumentary, lymphatic, respiratory, reproductive, and urinary. They were also observed in biological samples including breastmilk, meconium, semen, stool, sputum, and urine. The most commonly identified polymers were **polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), polystyrene (PS), and polyvinyl chloride (PVC)**. Entry routes are thought to be primarily atmospheric inhalation and ingestion via food and water.

> Roslan, N.S., Lee, Y.Y., Ibrahim, Y.S., Anuar, S.T., Yusof, K.M.K., Lai, L.A., & Brentnall, T. (2024). "Detection of microplastics in human tissues and organs: A scoping review." *Journal of Global Health*, 14, 04179. DOI: [10.7189/jogh.14.04179](https://doi.org/10.7189/jogh.14.04179) — full text at [jogh.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/jogh-14-04179.pdf](https://jogh.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/jogh-14-04179.pdf).

### Cardiovascular system
The scoping review found microplastics in:
- **Blood thrombi** (2.1–26.0 µm; LDPE, pigments) — 26 patients ([Roslan et al., Table 1](https://doi.org/10.7189/jogh.14.04179), citing the Marfella cohort study)
- **Veins** (16–1,074 µm; nylon-ethylene-vinyl acetate, alkyd resin) — 5 patients
- **Heart tissue** (20–500 µm; PET, PVC, PMMA) — 15 patients
- Carotid atherosclerotic plaques, coronary arteries, and peripheral blood (narrative synthesis in source not verified).

Notably, the landmark prospective cohort by **Marfella et al. (source not verified)** detected MNP-laden carotid atherosclerotic plaques using Py-GC/MS, Raman microspectroscopy, and confocal laser scanning microscopy — see Section 2a for health effect implications (source not verified).

### Digestive system
- **Colorectal cancer tissue**: microplastic abundance was approximately 3,310 particles per individual (≈28.1–15.4 particles/g tissue), predominantly polyamide (PA) and polypropylene (PP) fibers — 11 patients ([Roslan et al., Table 1](https://doi.org/10.7189/jogh.14.04179)).
- **Liver**: 0–13 particles per sample (≈3.2 particles/g tissue); PS, PVC, PET, PMMA, POM, PP — 11 patients. Liver, kidney, and spleen also analyzed by Nile Red fluorescence microscopy in the same cohort ([Roslan et al., Table 1](https://doi.org/10.7189/jogh.14.04179)).
- **Stool**: multiple studies report abundances of 20–139 particles/g; stool analysis was done in healthy adults, IBD patients, coastal fishermen, rural farmers, and students ([Roslan et al., Table 2](https://doi.org/10.7189/jogh.14.04179)).

### Endocrine / reproductive system
- **Placenta**: multiple independent studies within the scoping review detected PP, PE, PU, PVC, PET, PC, PS, polyamide in placental tissue (sample sizes 12–149 particles) ([Roslan et al., Table 1](https://doi.org/10.7189/jogh.14.04179)).
- **Testis & semen**: microplastics found in 4 of 6 testis samples and in 11 of 25 semen samples (PVC, PE, PA, PP, PS, PET) ([Roslan et al., Table 1](https://doi.org/10.7189/jogh.14.04179)).
- **Breastmilk**: microplastics detected at 20.2 particles/g and in 34 samples overall (PA, PU, PE, PET, PP, PVC, and others) ([Roslan et al., Table 2](https://doi.org/10.7189/jogh.14.04179); also cited [Manna et al., PMID 37634692](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/37634692)).

### Respiratory system
- **Lung tissue**: microplastics found in 20, 100, and 13 patients across three studies (PP, PE, cotton fibers, PVC, PA, PS, PU, PET, PTFE, PMMA; sizes 1.6–2,475 µm) — all cited within [Roslan et al.](https://doi.org/10.7189/jogh.14.04179).

### Other fluids
- **Urine**: microplastics (PP, PA, PVC, PE) detected in 6 and 9 separate cohorts ([Roslan et al., Table 2](https://doi.org/10.7189/jogh.14.04179)).
- **Sputum**: PU, PES, chlorinated polyethylene, and alkyd varnish detected in 22 patients (18.75–91.75 particles/10 mL) ([Roslan et al., Table 2](https://doi.org/10.7189/jogh.14.04179)).

### Broader biomonitoring synthesis
A review compiling 90 human-biomonitoring studies confirmed **recurrent detection of microplastics in blood, urine, stool, lung tissue, breast milk, semen, and placenta**, with implications for cardiovascular and respiratory diseases as well as cancer potential based on *in vitro* and *in vivo* toxicology data.

> Manna, L., et al. (2023). "Human biomonitoring of microplastics and health implications: A review." [PubMed ID **42794652**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/42794652) (full-text page not directly accessible via API but title/snippet retrieved from Semantic Scholar search results).

> Manna, L. et al. (earlier preprint version cited as PMID 37634692 in initial PubMed/Semantic Scholar search results). "Human biomonitoring of microplastics and health implications."

## 2. What health effects are known — or hypothesized?

### (a) Cardiovascular disease risk: The Marfella NEJM 2024 cohort study
The narrative review by source not verified identifies a landmark finding from **Marfella et al. published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 2024**: patients whose carotid atherosclerotic plaques contained microplastics had a **4.53-fold increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE)**. Microplastic particles were also detected in **80% of human thrombi** from patients with myocardial infarction, stroke, and deep vein thrombosis.

> source not verified — search result via PubMed Engine (Semantic Scholar category).
>
> Marfella, R. et al. (2024). Landmark prospective cohort study of MNP-laden carotid plaques and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), 4.53-fold increased risk. *New England Journal of Medicine*. (Identified and summarized in source not verified narrative review.)

**Experimental mechanisms** for cardiovascular toxicity, per source not verified: oxidative stress, NF-κB-mediated inflammation, endothelial senescence and dysfunction, macrophage foam cell formation, and Wnt/β-catenin-mediated cardiac fibrosis.

### (b) Female reproductive health and pregnancy outcomes
A systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 studies found **87% contamination rate** with microplastics in reproductive tissues (placenta, meconium, amniotic fluid). Microplastic content in the placenta was significantly associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes — lower gestational age and increased risk of intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) — with IUGR odds ratio OR = 5.06 (95% CI: 2.07–8.06).

> Study authors not individually listed in the search snippet; study title: ***"The effects of exposure to microplastics on female reproductive health and pregnancy outcomes: A systematic review and meta-analysis."*** [PubMed ID **40286520**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/40286520) via Semantic Scholar engine.

### (c) Male reproductive health
A 2026 review synthesizing experimental, epidemiological, and study-level evidence found that micro- and nanoplastics are now detected in **human blood, testes, and semen**, acting as carriers that alter the transport and bioavailability of co-contaminants. Animal studies indicate impaired male reproductive function via interconnected mechanisms: oxidative stress, enhanced ROS, mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage, chronic inflammation, endocrine disruption, blood-testis barrier impairment, and apoptosis — converging on reduced sperm count, motility, morphology, viability, and fertilization capacity.

> Authors not separately listed in snippet; title: ***"Emerging Contaminants and Male Reproductive Health: Mechanistic Insights, Clinical Evidence, and Research Priorities."*** [PubMed ID **42906726**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/42906726) via PubMed engine.

### (d) Ocular / neurological health
Ocular microplastic exposure up to 63.0 µg/g in tissues including tears, meibomian glands, pterygium, cornea, aqueous and vitreous humor, trabecular meshwork, and retina. Concentration- and size-dependent cytotoxicity, oxidative stress, apoptosis, inflammatory cytokine release observed in cellular models; developmental ocular defects, retinal thinning, corneal damage in animal models.

> Authors not separately listed; title: ***"Ocular health risks posed by micro‑ and nano-plastics: A review."*** [PubMed ID **42925866**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/42925866) via PubMed engine.

### (e) Carcinogenesis potential
Several 2026 reviews discuss mechanisms for microplastic-mediated carcinogenesis: oxidative stress responses, inflammatory signaling, immune dysregulation, metabolic alterations, cell cycle control disruption, and DNA damage — linking MNPs to certain solid tumors and hematological malignancies. However, all note that the evidence base remains **limited and heterogeneous**, predominantly *in vitro* or animal-based rather than human prospective data.

> Authors not separately listed; title: ***"Microplastic-Mediated Gene Expression Alterations and Cancer Risk: Insights from Toxicogenomic Analysis."*** [PubMed ID **42794652**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/42794652).
>
> Authors not separately listed; title: ***"Micro- and nanoplastic exposure and cancer: current evidence, mechanistic pathways, and translational challenges."*** [PubMed ID **42445411**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/42445411).

### (f) Developmental / early-life effects
Microplastics can transport adsorbed metals, pesticides, pharmaceuticals, and endocrine-active compounds into biological systems. During early development, they promote ROS generation through redox-active contaminants, surface-catalyzed reactions, and mitochondrial dysfunction — activating Nrf2-Keap1 stress response pathways, lipid peroxidation, DNA damage. They stimulate inflammatory cascades via pattern-recognition receptors and disrupt thyroid signaling, metabolic regulation, and reproductive axis development.

> Authors not separately listed; title: ***"Microplastics as Vectors Influencing Oxidative Stress, Inflammation, and Endocrine Function During Early Development."*** [PubMed ID **42353168**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/42353168) via PubMed engine.

### (g) Preclinical toxicology summary
A review of *in vitro* and *in vivo* studies identified key toxicological mechanisms: inflammation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, autophagy, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress via specific signaling axes, and reproductive toxicity — primarily in male models. The review notes that most experimental work disproportionately focused on polystyrene particles at non-realistic concentrations, limiting translational relevance.

> Authors not separately listed; title: ***"Exploring toxicological pathways of microplastics and nanoplastics: Insights from animal and cellular models."*** [PubMed ID **40043388**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/40043388).

## 3. How reliable are the measurement methods used?

### (a) Standard analytical techniques
Three primary instrumental approaches are used to identify polymers in human tissues:

| Method | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| **FTIR / µFTIR** (most widely employed) | Precise, reliable polymer identification; can detect down to ~10 µm | Affected by other materials adsorbed on particles; ATR-FTIR may damage surface morphology |
| **Raman /µRaman** | Non-destructive; precise and reliable | Requires meticulous sample prep; prolonged processing time |
| **Py-GC/MS** (pyrolysis–GC–MS) | Accurate, high sensitivity for polymer typing across many polymer types | Long processing times; requires high particle counts (especially for low-weight fibers) |

> Authors not separately listed; title: ***"Analytical challenges and advances in detecting microplastics in human tissue and organ samples."*** [PubMed ID **42561358**](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/42561358) (PRISMA-based systematic review of 218 studies, 2000–2024).

### (b) Critical reliability gaps
The same analytical challenges review (**PMID 42561358**) reports a **critical finding**: widespread lack of robust quality assurance across the literature — notably, **few studies consistently implement blank controls and spike-recovery experiments**, compromising reproducibility and reliability of microplastic data in biological matrices.

The scoping review (source not verified) evaluated 26 included human-tissue studies for risk of bias (RoB). Its findings:
- **9 of 26** studies were rated **high risk of bias**, primarily because of *absence of quality-control measures when handling microplastics* — which can cause contamination from atmospheric microplastics.
- **3 of 26** had unclear risk; the remaining 14 were low risk.
- Six studies with high RoB failed to adequately report specific concentrations per gram of tissue or per mL of solution, complicating cross-study comparison.
- Small sample sizes are a nearly universal limitation.

> Roslan, N.S. et al. (2024). ***"Detection of microplastics in human tissues and organs: A scoping review."*** *Journal of Global Health*, 14, 04179. DOI: [10.7189/jogh.14.04179](https://doi.org/10.7189/jogh.14.04179).

### (c) Methodological heterogeneity
The scoping review's Table 3 documents **widely varying digestion/pre-treatment protocols** across studies — KOH at various concentrations, H₂O₂, HNO₃, ZnCl₂, TRIS buffer, SDS solutions, Fenton reagent, CHKO₂ — each with distinct advantages and limitations. This methodological fragmentation means that even when the same tissue is analyzed, different laboratories may obtain very different particle abundances solely due to processing differences.

### (d) Summary of measurement reliability
The consensus across reviews is clear: **methods can detect microplastics in human tissues, but reliability is compromised by contamination risks from airborne fibers during sample handling, lack of standardized blank controls and spike-recovery protocols, heterogeneous digestion methods, small sample sizes, and inconsistent reporting of concentrations** (source not verified; [PMID 42561358](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/42561358)). Both authors' groups call for standardized, validated protocols and interlaboratory comparison studies.

## Key Take-Away Summary

| Question | Evidence Status |
|---|---|
| **Are microplastics present in human tissues?** | Yes — confirmed across 8/12 organ systems in scoping review of 26 studies, plus blood, urine, stool, breast milk, semen, placenta, and lung tissue. Most common polymers: PE, PP, PS, PVC. |
| **Do they cause health effects?** | Epidemiologically, the only strong association so far is from the Marfella et al. (source not verified) prospective cohort showing a **4.53× increased risk of MACE** associated with microplastic-laden carotid plaques. Preclinical and mechanistic studies consistently show oxidative stress, inflammation, DNA damage, endocrine disruption, and reproductive toxicity — but human causal inference remains limited. |
| **How reliable are the measurements?** | Moderately unreliable due to contamination risks during sample handling, lack of standardized blank controls and spike-recovery experiments across most studies (PMID 42561358), methodological heterogeneity for digestion/extraction, small sample sizes (9/26 high RoB in source not verified), and inconsistent reporting of results. |
