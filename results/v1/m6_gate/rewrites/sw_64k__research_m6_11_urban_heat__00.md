## Urban Heat Island (UHI) — Summary from the Full Wikipedia Article

### Definition & Scope
An **urban heat island** is the relative warmth of cities compared to surrounding rural areas, caused by land-use modification, the configuration of the built environment, heat-absorbing building materials, reduced ventilation and greenery, and direct emissions of waste heat from human activities. Urban areas host over half of the world's population while occupying only about 0.5% of Earth's land surface [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-Phelan-1).

### Diurnal & Seasonal Patterns
- **Diurnal**: Urban surfaces warm faster during the day due to high heat capacities (e.g., concrete holds ~2,000× as much heat as an equivalent volume of air) and later release stored warmth at night [[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-15). In the U.S., urban daytime temperatures exceed rural by 1–7°F (0.6–3.9°C), while nighttime difference is 2–5°F (1.1–2.8°C) [[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-:22-17). The nighttime effect is particularly pronounced because no solar heating occurs, trapping warm air near still-warm surfaces.
- **Seasonal**: Seasonal UHI variability is "less well understood than diurnal variability" and involves complex interactions between precipitation, vegetation, solar radiation, and surface materials across local climate zones [[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-:8-23).

### Measurements
The **UHI Index (UHII)**, created by California EPA in 2015, compares hourly temperatures at 2 m above ground between a city and upwind rural reference points, summing degree-Celsius-hours. It estimates expected air-conditioning use and greenhouse gas emissions but excludes wind speed, humidity, and solar influx [[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-27). The first comprehensive numerical UHI model was published by **Leonard O. Myrup in 1969** [[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-:3-33).

### Major Findings on Aspects of Social Inequality

#### 1. Racial/Ethnic Disparities
A study by climatologist **Angel Hsu and colleagues** found that "the average person of color lives in a census tract with higher SUHI intensity than non-Hispanic whites in all but 6 of the 175 largest urbanized areas" in the U.S. [[135]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-:6a-135). Similarly, **communities of color** are disproportionately affected, raising environmental justice concerns [[124]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-Diaz_article-124), [[129]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-Teron-129).

#### 2. Income Inequality & Tree Canopy
There is a strong correlation between neighborhood income and tree canopy: low-income neighborhoods have significantly fewer trees, partly because they lack financial resources for planting/maintenance while affluent communities afford more on public and private property [[130]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-Demand-130), [[131]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-Income_Inequality-131). Lower-income households are also less likely to afford air conditioning, compounding heat vulnerability.

#### 3. Redlining & Historical Urban Heat Exposure
Formerly **redlined neighborhoods** in 108 U.S. cities are on average **2.6°C (4.7°F) hotter** than non-redlined areas, primarily due to a lack of tree canopy and more heat-absorbing surfaces [[138]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-138). In Durham, Fresno, and Pittsburgh "D" graded areas have substantially lower tree coverage than wealthy "A" neighborhoods (predominantly white) [[139]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-139). Phoenix's formerly redlined communities reach **5.6–8.3°C (10–15°F)** higher temperatures [[140]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-140). A 2023 policy brief found that historically redlined and low-income Black neighborhoods also experience clusters of **violent crime**, amplifying intersecting vulnerabilities [[129]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-Teron-129).

Additional research in China showed urban haze from air pollution contributes up to **0.7°C** of additional nighttime warming, linking atmospheric processes and neighborhood heat inequity [[142]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-142).

#### 4. Global South Disparities
A study by **Kanging Huang and colleagues** found that heat exposure effects have increased faster in the Global South in recent decades, per [133]], with a World Bank study finding a **7.0°C disparity** between hottest and coolest neighborhoods in Bandung, Indonesia [[134]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-134).

#### 5. Housing Conditions & "Space Poverty"
Top-floor living with dark roofs and poor insulation exacerbates heating for lower-income individuals [[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-:1a-52). In **Hong Kong**, "space poverty" describes extremely small sub-divided units lacking ventilation; residents seek libraries, parks, and cafés for cooling relief [[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-:1a-52).

#### 6. African American Health Vulnerability
Per **Professor Pamela Jackson and colleagues**, African Americans have higher chronic disease rates (asthma, diabetes), which are exacerbated by extreme heat, leading to hypertension or stroke risk [[136]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-136).

#### 7. Demographic Heat Stress Vulnerability
High-vulnerability groups include the elderly (unable to regulate body temperature, pre-existing conditions), women, children (developing bodies), low-income households, and chronically ill [[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-:1a-52).

### Impacts on Multiple Systems
- **Weather/Climate**: Rainfall rates downwind of cities increase by 48–116%; monthly rainfall is 28% greater 20–40 miles downwind [[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-55), [[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-56).
- **Human Health**: UHIs increase heat illnesses (stroke, exhaustion, syncope, cramps) and mortality [[63]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-Review1-63). Extreme urban heat correlates with increased ozone and pollutants (VOCs, CO, NOx, PM) [[68]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-DOE-68), [[69]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-WHO-69). Poor ventilation correlates with stronger UHI and higher mortality in Hong Kong [[70]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-70), [[71]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-71).
- **Water/Aquatic Life**: Hot pavement heats stormwater, causing fish kills (e.g., Cedar Rapids, Iowa: 10.5°C rise in one hour killing ~188 fish) [[75]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-75).
- **Energy**: U.S. residents spend 15% of energy on air conditioning for buildings in heat islands; LA spent ~$100 million/year (2000) [[83]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-83).

### Mitigation Options
- **Street trees**: 2024 meta-analysis across 110 cities finds typical ~1–2°C cooling; locations with no canopy within 10 m were up to 5× more likely to exceed 32.2°C [[94]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-Li2024-94), [[95]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-Ettinger2024-95).
- **Cool roofs/paint**: A University College London study by Oscar Brousse found cool roofs (white-painted or reflective-coated) can reduce average outdoor temperatures by 1.2°C, up to 2°C in some areas — outperforming solar panels and tree cover [[88]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-auto-88).
- **Green infrastructure**: Includes green roofs (insulation, stormwater management), green parking lots, and urban green-blue grids that provide co-benefits: heat reduction, biodiversity, air quality, food production, recreation [[113]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-Green_infrastructure_Urban_green-blue_grids-113), [[116]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-Green_infrastructure_issuu.com-116).
- **Passive daytime radiative cooling (PDRC)**: Can double energy savings of white roofs; highest potential in hot/dry cities like Phoenix and Las Vegas [[118]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-:352-118), [[119]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-:36-119).

### Social & Policy Response
Beginning in the 2020s, cities have increasingly appointed **Chief Heat Officers** to coordinate anti-heat efforts (shade, cooling centers, tree planting). Early positions were created in Los Angeles, Miami-Dade, Melbourne, Athens, and Freetown, organized by the Adrienne Arsht–Rockefeller Foundation Resilience Center [[145]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-145), [[147]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island#cite_note-147). Some cities now use equity indexes (e.g., LA Equity Index) to ensure heat-mitigation programs address historically disadvantaged communities.
