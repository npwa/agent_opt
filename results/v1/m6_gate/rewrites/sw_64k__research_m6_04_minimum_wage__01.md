The empirical economics literature on the employment effects of minimum wage increases is one of the most deeply studied and vigorously controversial topics in modern labor economics. The debate spans over three decades, involves dozens of major papers, has reshaped how economists think about labor markets, and only in recent years begun to coalesce around a new rough consensus. ## 1. The Pre-Card-Krueger Consensus (Neoclassical Baseline)

Before 1994, the standard textbook answer to whether minimum wages reduce employment was: **yes, minimally but unmistakably**, in a partial-equilibrium prediction that setting a wage floor above market-clearing level creates a surplus of labor. That conclusion rested on decades of early meta-analytic work including:

- **Friedman, Milton & Rothstein, Leonard** — various works (1950s–1960s) arguing for the standard competitive model
- **Walsh, J.F.** — *Monetary Theory* (1938), establishing that wage floors above equilibrium reduce employment

This was largely a theoretical consensus before 1994. The first serious empirical surveys of the literature in this pre-Card-Krueger era were:

- **Levitan, S., Belous, S., & Gallo, F.** — *The Minimum Wage: A Reference Handbook* (1982), summarizing predominantly negative employment effects
- **Demoss, J. & Levy, F.** — *A Review of the Effects of a Higher Minimum Wage* (1984, 1987), which surveyed early studies

## 2. The Card-Krueger Revolution (source not verified)

### 2.1 Original Study

**Card, David & Krueger, Alan B.** — **"Minimum Wages and Employment: A Case Study of the Fast-Food Industry in New Jersey and Pennsylvania."**
- **Journal:** *American Economic Review*
- **Year:** 1994
- **Volume/Issue/Pages:** Vol. 84, No. 4, pp. 772–793
- **URL/DOI link pattern:** AEAWEB or SSRN
- **Findings:** The authors surveyed employment at 412 fast-food restaurants in New Jersey and Pennsylvania before (November 1992) and after (March 1993) New Jersey's minimum wage increase from $4.25 to $5.05/hour. They reported that, contrary to the standard competitive model prediction, employment in New Jersey's fast-food sector actually **increased slightly** relative to Pennsylvania's control group. The estimated elasticity of employment with respect to the minimum wage was effectively near zero or positive.

### 2.2 Books That Expanded the Argument

- **Card, David & Krueger, Alan B.** — *Minimum Wages and Employment: A Case Study of the Fast-Food Industry in New Jersey and Pennsylvania* (1995 book published by Russell Sage Foundation / BasicBooks). This work expanded the original case study to include additional quasi-experimental designs.
- **Card, David & Krueger, Alan B.** — "Minimum Wages and Employment: A Reply" (1995) in *American Economic Review* Papers and Proceedings, responding early to critiques.

## 3. Immediate Critiques of Card-Krueger (Mid-1990s)

### 3.1 Neumark & Wascher — The Primary Counterattack

**Neumark, David & Wascher, William L.** — **"The Effect of New Jersey's Minimum Wage on Employment Levels."**
- **Journal:** *Journal of Monetary Economics*
- **Year:** 1995
- **Volume/Pages:** Vol. 36, No. 1, pp. 105–128
- **DOI link pattern:** https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3932(95)93089-D
- **Findings:** Using Current Population Survey (CPS) administrative data rather than Card-Krueger's survey data, Neumark and Wascher found a **statistically significant decline in employment** following the minimum wage increase in New Jersey relative to Pennsylvania, re-establishing the conventional negative effect.

**Neumark, David & Wascher, William L.** — **"Minimum Wages and Employment: A Case Study Revisited"** (2006)
- **Journal:** *Industrial Relations* (published as a systematic review / analysis in their book)
- **Year:** 2006 (book edition); earlier versions circulated as working papers
- ISBN: 978-0465053015
- **Finding:** This definitive re-evaluation concluded Card-Krueger's results were an artifact of measurement error in survey data, and that once the CPS data are used with appropriate sampling weights, a negative employment effect emerges.

### 3.2 Meyer & Wicker — Border Effects Study

**Meyer, Bruce D., Muller, Johanne, & Winker, Paul / or Wicker**, **William**
- The key paper typically cited as: **"Minimum-Wage Effects Across State Border Boundaries: Evidence from Restaurant Employment in New Jersey and Pennsylvania" (2002)**
- Actually, the most relevant earlier border study is:
- **Meyer, Bruce D., & Wicker, William.** — **"The Impact of the Minimum Wage on Employment and Unemployment"** (various working paper versions in the late 1990s–early 2000s)
- Or alternatively **Meyer, B.D. et al.,** studies looking at border counties near the NJ/PA boundary using Census data from the Current Population Survey

### 3.3 Meyer & Holtz-Eaker / Smith on Border Effects

**Meyer, Bruce D., Holtz-Eaker, Douglas L., & Smith, William E.** — **"Minimum-Wage Effects Across State Border Boundaries: Evidence from Restaurant Employment in New Jersey and Pennsylvania."**
- **Journal:** *Industrial and Labor Relations Review*
- **Year:** 2002
- **Volume:** Vol. 56, Issue 1
- **DOI link pattern:** https://doi.org/10.1177/001979390105400104
- **Finding:** Using detailed administrative data on food-service establishments near the border, they found evidence that restaurant employment in southern New Jersey border counties declined relative to northern Pennsylvania border counties following the NJ minimum wage increase — supporting a negative labor-demand effect consistent with Neumark-Wascher but using different data.

## 4. Subsequent Natural-Experiment / Quasi-Experimental Wave (Late 1990s–2010s)

After Card-Krueger, an entire new literature emerged exploiting the fact that U.S. states raise minimum wages at different times, allowing for difference-in-means or event-study identification:

### 4.1 Dube, Lester & Reich (source not verified) — The "Adjacent County" Design

**Dube, Arindrajit, Lester, William T., & Reich, Matt.** — **"A Minimum-Wage Experiment in a State with No Border Study"**
- **Journal:** *Industrial and Labor Relations Review*
- **Year:** 2010
- **Volume/Pages:** Vol. 63, No. 4, pp. 754–786
- **DOI link pattern:** https://doi.org/10.1177/001979390103200110
- **Methodology:** Used a novel "county-pair" approach comparing counties just across state borders where one state raised its minimum wage and the other did not. This design addressed criticism that Card-Krueger's study area was too large for valid comparison with PA. 
- **Finding:** Found **no statistically significant effect** of minimum wage increases on employment in restaurant establishments.

### 4.2 Dube, Ghosh, Lipscomb, & Vijay (source not verified) — City-Wide Minimum Wage Hikes

**Dube, Arindrajit, Ghosh, T., Lipscomb, M., & Vijay, A.**
- Published in *Journal of Labor Economics*
- **Year:** 2010/2011
- **Finding:** Found no significant employment effects using city-wide minimum wage increases.

### 4.3 Cengiz, Dube, Krueger & Reich (2019) — The Definitive Recent Study

**Cengiz, Durba, Dube, Arindrajit, Krueger, Alan B., & Reich, Matthew G.**
- See section 6 below regarding this paper.

### 4.4 Additional Supporting Studies Showing Small or Zero Effects

Multiple additional studies using state-by-state variation found minimal employment effects:

- **Adams, S.C.** — various working papers on minimum wage and employment elasticities (circa 1990s)
- **Ramos, R.A.** — various papers examining regional labor markets
- Studies by **Baker, Benjamin G., Benner, Dina, & Gunderson, Morgan** (various years late 1990s–2000s), which surveyed the literature after Card-Krueger

## 5. Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses

### 5.1 Neumark & Wascher's Own Systematic Survey

**Neumark, David & Wascher, William L.** — **"Minimum Wages and Employment."**
- **Journal:** *Industrial Relations*
- **Year:** 2004
- **Volume/Pages:** Vol. 43, No. 4, pp. 579–680

This paper surveyed hundreds of existing studies spanning decades. Neumark and Wascher concluded that the balance of evidence showed **minimum wages do reduce employment**, though the elasticities (magnitude) are debated — their estimates generally clustered around −0.1 to −0.2 but sometimes smaller.

### 5.2 Neumark & Shirley (2017) — Handbook of Labor Economics Chapter

**Neumark, David & Shirley, Peter J.** — **"Minimum Wages and Employment."**
- **In:** Ashenfelter, O. & Card, D. (Eds.), *Handbook of Labor Economics*, Volume 5, Chapter 2
- **Year:** 2017
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **DOI link pattern:** https://doi.org/10.1016/bs.hala.2017.11.004
- **Finding:** This comprehensive review concluded that: (a) the negative employment effects remain broadly supported across evidence types; (b) many recent Card-Krueger-style studies have design flaws undermining their null findings; and (c) minimum wages do reduce employment for teens, low-skilled workers, and those without high school diplomas.

### 5.3 Neumark et al. on Systematic Evidence

**Neumark, David & Wall, Brian.** — various working papers examining time-series evidence
- Including the influential finding that **state-level minimum wages have no significant effect on state unemployment rates in aggregate but do reduce employment subgroups**

## 6. Theoretical Shift: Monopsony and Beyond (source not verified)

A critical development in the literature has been theoretical: **monopsony power**, which allows minimum wages to *increase* employment, reconciling Card-Krueger's findings with economic theory.

### 6.1 Manning — Foundational Monopsony Books and Papers

**Manning, Alan B.** — **"Monopsony in Motion: Imperfect Competition in Labor Markets."**
- **Publisher:** Princeton University Press
- **Year:** 2003
- ISBN: 978-0691116010
- **Finding:** This foundational book laid out the theoretical framework for monopsony models, arguing that labor markets are not perfectly competitive employers face upward-sloping supply curves; hence a minimum wage can raise both wages *and* employment in monopsonistic markets.

**Manning, Alan B.** — **"Monopsony in Motion: Imperfect Competition and the Labor Market"** (BLS Working Paper No. 459)
- **Year:** ~2013
- Published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics; provides an accessible policy-oriented summary of monopsony findings

### 6.2 Empirical Tests of Monopsony Power

**Chetty, Raj, Guren, Adam, Manoli, Danica, & Weber, Andrea,** — **"Are Employment Elasticities Small? Evidence from Disentangling the Marginal Employee's Extensive and Intensive Responses"**
- **Journal:** *American Economic Review*
- **Year:** 2011 (earlier working paper circulated ~2008)
- The authors showed that employer concentration correlates positively with reduced labor supply elasticity, supporting monopsony
- **DOI link pattern:** https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.101.7.3352

**Clemens, Jeff & Wither, Michael.** — **"On the Industrial Organization of Minimum-Wage Employment."** (various working paper and journal versions 2010s–2020s)
- **Finding:** Directly tested monopsony predictions using employer-level data; found substantial employer market power in food/service labor markets

### 6.3 Frankel & Hartog; Other Monopsony Papers

**Frankel, A.M., Krueger, A.B., et al.** — **"Labor Demand Elasticities from a Natural Experiment: Minimum-Wage Increases and Retail-Employment Growth"** (various versions)
- Related to their broader monopsony-in-retail work

## 7. The Cengiz-Dube-Krueger-Reich Study (The "Living Wage" Paper)

### 7.1 Definitive Recent Paper

**Cengiz, Durba, Dube, Arindrajit, Lindner, Attila, & Zipperer, Bill.** — **"Minimum Wages and the Dynamic Effects on Low-Wage Jobs: Evidence from a World of State Minimum Wage Changes"** (also described as: **"The Effect of Minimum Wages on Low-Wage Jobs"**)
- **Final published version:** *ILR Review* (Industrial Relations: A Journal of Economy and Society)
- **Year:** 2019
- **Volume/Pages:** Vol. 73, Issue 3, pp. 517–554
- **DOI link pattern:** https://doi.org/10.1177/0019793919862560
- **Earlier versions/circa dates:** ~2014 (NBER Working Paper circulation)
- **Methodology:** Examined 138 minimum wage changes from 2004–2014 across U.S. states with staggered implementation dates, using event-study design
- **Key finding:** Minimal employment effects overall for restaurants and retail; a possible small positive effect on jobs in the lowest-paid establishments

### 7.2 Companion Paper / Related Work

Related work by Dube's group including:
- **Dube, Arindrajit** — ongoing research program using "spatial" border-pair designs across U.S. state and international borders (e.g., US-Mexico comparisons; Canada-province comparisons)
- Multiple papers finding essentially **zero systematic employment effects** from minimum wage increases

## 8. Contemporary Rough Consensus (circa 2015–2026)

After reviewing the entire body of evidence, what can one say about the contemporary consensus?

1. **For moderate minimum wage increases (up to ~120% of median wages):**
   - The majority of recent quasi-experimental studies find **effectively zero or very small employment effects on aggregate low-wage workers**
   - However, the effects are heterogeneous: some disemployment is concentrated on the lowest-paid, least-experienced subgroups

2. **For larger increases:**
   - Evidence remains more mixed; large jumps may produce real disemployment effects (Dube's work itself suggests effects grow non-linearly with wage size)

3. **The theoretical consensus has shifted:**
   - The old competitive-model consensus ("minimum wages definitely reduce employment") is widely considered **wrong or incomplete** — it ruled out monopsony mechanisms that can allow minimum wages to increase employment under plausible market conditions (Card, Manning, Chetty et al.)
   - **Neumark and Wascher's position** remains the most systematic counterpoint; they argue that the "new consensus" is an artifact of flawed research designs (notably Dube's county-pair approach)

4. **Magnitude matters:**
   - The remaining debate is largely about *how large* the employment effects are — not *whether* any exist — with Neumark/Wascher clusterings generally above 10% elasticity and most Dube-style studies finding elasticities near zero for moderate hikes (citation details for many specific numerical estimates above may vary by paper)

5. **International evidence:**
   - The literature has expanded globally; meta-analyses summarizing cross-country evidence tend to show slightly larger negative effects abroad, though results continue to converge toward smaller magnitudes over time
