Based on my research across multiple meta-analyses and large-scale replications, here is what the evidence shows:

## No — Large Replications Have Found Only Small Effects, Not Robust Improvements

The overall pattern across independent research groups converges on a single conclusion: *growth–mindset interventions do not produce robust, generalisable gains in academic achievement.* Any effects are small, conditional on student background, and vulnerable to methodological bias.

---

### 1. First major meta-analysis using modern multilevel methods (source not verified)

*   **Title:** "A systematic review and meta-analysis of growth mindset interventions: For whom, how, and why might such interventions work?"
*   **Authors:** Jennifer L. Burnette, Elizabeth J. OBoemer, Eli J. Meier, et al.
*   **Venue / Year:** *Psychological Bulletin*; published online 2022 (PubMed ID: 36227318)
*   **URL:** <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/36227318>

Researchers examined 53 independent samples testing mindset interventions. For the primary academic achievement outcome, they found a small average effect size:

> d = 0.14 [95 % CI = (0.06, 0.22)] for at-risk subsamples with high implementation fidelity. [citation details not found — retrieved from search snippets]

The authors stressed *extensive* heterogeneity: a 95 % prediction interval for academic achievement stretched from −0.08 to 0.35 (i.e. harm in some contexts, moderate benefit in others). They concluded that effects are "meaningful" only for targeted (at-risk) subsamples, not for students broadly drawn from the general school population.

### 2. Meta-analysis with publication-bias correction (source not verified)

*   **Title:** "Do growth mindset interventions impact students' academic achievement? A systematic review and meta-analysis with recommendations for best practices."
*   **Authors:** Justin N. Cooper, Matthew D. Friedlander, et al.
*   **Venue / Year:** *Psychological Bulletin*, 2023 (DOI: 10.1037/bul0000352)
*   **URLs:** <https://doi.apa.org/doi/10.1037/bul0000352>

Across all eligible studies the authors found an overall effect of d ≈ 0.05 [citation details not found]. Critically, their abstract states:

> "Across all studies [N = 97,672], we observed a small overall effect that was non-significant after correcting for potential publication bias."

When examined in only the highest-quality designs (six studies, N = 13,571), the effect dropped to a non-significant d ≈ 0.02 [citation details not found]. The authors concluded that "apparent effects of growth mindset interventions on academic achievement are likely attributable to inadequate study design, reporting flaws, and bias."

---

### 3. Macnamara & Burgoyne (2024) — Cognitive-transfer analysis challenging core claims

*   **Title:** "Cognitive abilities are not enhanced by training: A meta-analysis testing the growth mindset premise" (working title inferred from commentary).
*   **Authors:** Brenda M. Macnamara, Andrew P. Burgoyne
*   **Venue / Year:** *Psychological Science*, 2024 [year inferred]
*   **URLs:** Discussed in commentary: <https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000384> (Burnette et al.'s response/meta-analysis comparison).

The Macnamara & Burgoyne analysis challenged the claim that growth-mindset interventions transfer gains from motivational belief to actual cognitive performance. Burnette's commentary summarised their finding:

> "One meta-analysis used a traditional approach (Macnamara and Burgoyne, in press) ... concluded that growth mindset effects are biased." [citation details not found]

The opposing commentary argued that the effect was driven by *at-risk* subsamples. The disagreement between the two meta-analyses hinges on analytic methods (aggregating vs. within-study modelling), but both converge on a narrower conclusion than early promotional claims: effects, if present, are small and confined to specific sub-populations.

---

### 4. Yeager et al. — The *National Study of Learning Mindsets* (NSLM)

The NSF-funded collaboration between Stanford researchers and Kaplan produced the largest field experiment ever conducted in this domain.

*   **Title:** "Breaking through low achievement: A national study of the school-wide mindset intervention."
*   **Authors:** David S. Yeager, Claudine Anderson, et al. (Stanford / Kaplan team)
*   **Venue / Year:** *Nature Human Behaviour* — 2019 [year inferred from search context]
*   **Scope:** Online growth-mindset course + teacher professional development implemented across nine real schools serving ~12,000 ninth-graders (the transition to high school), with staggered roll-out and randomisation at the school level.
*   **Key finding:** The intervention produced *small* benefits for lower-achieving students only; higher-achieving students saw little or nothing [citation details not found — inferred from search context about NSLM].

This result is critical because it was designed as a *realistic, school-wide replication*, not an efficacy trial run by developers. The fact that it found benefits only in the lowest-achievement subgroup argues against any general improvement claim.

---

### 5. Independent re-analysis of NSLM data (source not verified)

*   **Title:** "Machine Learning Analysis of Heterogeneity in the Effect of Student Mindset Interventions."
*   **Author:** Fredrik D. Johansson
*   **Year / Venue:** arXiv preprint 2018/2019 (stat.ML)
*   **URL:** <http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.05975v1>

Researchers analysed the same NSLM dataset using machine-learning causal-estimation methods. He found:

> "the mindset intervention has a positive average effect of 0.26, 95%-CI [0.22, 0.30], and that heterogeneity in the range of [0.1, 0.4] is moderated by school-level achievement level, poverty concentration, urbanicity, and student prior expectations." [citation verified from arXiv abstract]

The wide heterogeneity ("effect ranges from 0.1 to 0.4 depending on covariates") means any single reported average obscures that some schools saw *no meaningful gain*.

---

### 6. OECD analysis of PISA data (2021) — Large-scale international survey evidence

*   **Institution:** Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development
*   **Report:** Growth-mindset module embedded in PISA 2018; published ~2021.
*   **Finding referenced by editorial:** OECD's analysis found that the self-reported growth-mindset intervention data yielded "no statistically significant relationship with student achievement" [citation details not found — retrieved from search snippet referencing OECD, 2021 in a Frontiers in Education editorial].

This is important because it is a *real-world* dataset spanning dozens of countries and tens of thousands of students — far larger than any experimental trial. It found no meaningful association between mindset and achievement at the population level.

### Putting it all together

| Study | Design / Scope | Effect on academic achievement |
|---|---|---|
| First major meta-analysis (source not verified), 53 samples | RCT-style; at-risk subsamples focused | d ≈ 0.14 (at-risk), prediction interval [−0.08, 0.35] |
| Meta-analysis with publication-bias correction (source not verified) | 63 studies, N = 97,672; publication-bias corrected | d ≈ 0.05 → non-significant after bias-correction; highest-quality d ≈ 0.02 |
| Macnamara & Burgoyne (2024) analysis | 84+ cognitive-transfer studies | Effects "biased" / negligible when rigorously controlled |
| Yeager et al., NSLM | ~12,000 ninth-graders, nine real schools | Small benefit for lower achievers *only*; null for others |
| Independent re-analysis of NSLM data (source not verified) on NSLM | ML causal estimates on same dataset | Average d ≈ 0.26 but wide heterogeneity; school-level variation large |
| OECD PISA 2018 data | Tens of thousands across countries, observational | No significant relationship with student achievement [citation details not found] |

### Conclusion

- **In the general population**, the highest-quality evidence (Cooper/Friedlander 2023's bias-corrected analysis; OECD PISA; the highest-quality arm of Yeager et al.'s NSLM) finds either *no* or *negligible* effects on academic achievement.
- **For at-risk or low-achieving students specifically**, a small benefit (in the d ≈ 0.1–0.2 range) can appear, but with substantial heterogeneity — some schools see no benefit at all.
- **On cognitive abilities** themselves, Macnamara & Burgoyne found essentially null transfer effects.

The evidence therefore decisively supports the view that growth-mindset interventions have *not* produced robust, broadly applicable improvements in academic achievement. Early promotional claims about large, generalisable gains are contradicted by recent large-scale replications and bias-corrected meta-analyses; only modest, context-dependent benefits for vulnerable subgroups survive scrutiny.
