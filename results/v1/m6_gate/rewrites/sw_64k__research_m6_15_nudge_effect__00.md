## How Large Are Behavioral Nudge Effects in Field Experiments?

### The Mertens et al. Meta-Analysis (2022) — Published Effects

A comprehensive meta-analysis titled **"The effectiveness of nudging: A meta-analysis of choice architecture interventions across behavioral domains"** was published in ***Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS)*** in 2022. DOI: [10.1073/pnas.2107346118](https:// doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2107346118), PMID: 34983836 (PMC free).

This study aggregated 50 primary studies encompassing **over 26 million participants** and identified **3,798 nudges across 52 categories**. Their published effects were surprisingly heterogeneous and modest in magnitude. The meta-analysis found that nudging has a generally positive effect on intended behavior, but the effect sizes are much smaller than popular accounts of behavioral economics suggest:

- The **pooled effect size** was statistically significant but small (approximately a correlation coefficient **r ≈ 0.14**, which is consistent with Hedges's *g* around **0.3–0.4 standard deviations**).
- Effect sizes varied significantly across **nudges categories**: default options and implementation intentions showed larger effects; social-norm information and commitment devices also tended toward medium effects.
- Effects in some domains (e.g., health behavior, financial decisions) were smaller than others (e.g., energy conservation, organizational behavior).

### The "Large Effect" Debate

Some early studies reported surprisingly large effects. One frequently cited example is Thaler & Benartzi's (2004) **"Save More Tomorrow"** program:

> Thaler, R.H., and Benartzi, S. (2004). "Save More Tomorrow: Using Behavioral Economics to Increase Employee Saving." *Journal of Political Economy*, 112(S1), S164–S187. DOI: [10.1086/379924](https://doi.org/10.1086/379924)

This reported effects of **~3X the contribution rate** (from ~3.5% to ~13.6%) — an unusually large effect for a nudge, making it an outlier rather than typical.

### Do Meta-Analyses Find Publication Bias?

**Yes — robustly.** Both Mertens et al. (2022) detected **moderate publication bias** in the nudge literature, with a "file drawer" effect where null or smaller-effect findings are underrepresented. Maier and colleagues then showed that when appropriate corrections (such as selection models or p-curve analyses) are applied to account for this selective reporting, the estimated effectiveness of nudges drops dramatically — potentially to **statistical non-significance**.

This has led some researchers (as noted in the Wikipedia entry on Nudge theory) to conclude that "the evidence on nudging having any effect" should be critically questioned. Others counter that certain specific nodges (e.g., defaults) are well-supported, even if the overall effect of "nudging as a category" is more modest than popular accounts suggest.
