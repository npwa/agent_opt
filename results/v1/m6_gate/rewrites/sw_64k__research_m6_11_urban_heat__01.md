## What the Literature Says: Urban Green Space and Tree Cover as a Strategy to Reduce UHI Effects and Heat-Related Mortality

### 1. Urban Green Space and Tree Cover *do* Lower Urban Temperatures (UHI Mitigation)

The literature is remarkably consistent across cities, climates, methodologies, and continents: increasing urban greenness measurably reduces both surface and air temperatures through three primary biophysical mechanisms -- **shading**, **evapotranspiration**, and **albedo modification**. (Zheng et al., 2026; Lin & Li, 2025)

**Magnitude of cooling.** The quantitative evidence is compelling and cross-validating:

* Across seven U.S. cities using census-block-scale Landsat and landcover data, tree cover had an average cooling effect of **−0.089 K per % green cover**, approximately four times stronger than grass cover (−0.021 K per %) (source not verified), with cooling efficacy modulated by solar irradiance and vegetation moisture content (explaining 97% of the cooling variability). Albedo manipulations (e.g., cool roofs) offered consistent but less location-specific cooling at −0.187 K per 0.01 albedo increase.

* In a global analysis across **11,534 urban areas in 53 countries**, increasing greenness (by the Enhanced Vegetation Index, EVI) by 10% lowered warm-season mean temperatures by **0.08 °C**; by 20% lowered them by **0.14 °C**; and by 30% lowered them by **0.19 °C** (source not verified). These global reductions are modest per capita but translate into large mortality impacts because of the non-linear shape of heat-mortality dose-response curves at extreme temperatures.

* Across 232 Chinese cities (2001–2035), increasing urban green coverage by an average of **18.33%** was projected to reduce ambient temperatures by **0.20 °C** and potentially prevent approximately **19,764–26,360 annual deaths** from heatwave-attributable ischemic heart disease, stroke, and COPD (source not verified).

* A systematic review covering green roofs, street trees, urban parks, and vertical greening systems found that green areas can lower air and surface temperatures **by as much as 5 °C** within or adjacent to the green space itself. Cooling efficiency strongly depends on plant density, spatial distribution, and soil water availability (Lin & Li, 2025).

* In a compact Belgian city studied with 125 citizen-science weather stations over three years (2022–2024), daytime cooling by trees was quantified as the slope between tree-cover fraction and mean air temperature. Tree cooling **declined under humid atmospheric conditions, limited soil moisture, and high solar radiation coupled with low heat intensity** -- an important caveat for climate-change adaptation: *green space is less reliable when it is needed most during droughts.* Grasses and shrubs cooled more at night but also weakened under humidity and cloud cover. High soil moisture was essential to sustain cooling on the hottest days (source not verified).

* In Durham, North Carolina, using spatial causal inference on landcover data, hypothetical interventions including lining streets with trees were estimated to decrease evening temperatures by a maximum of **0.7–0.9 °C** per intervention, with diminishing returns at distance from the planted area (source not verified).

* In Bogor City, Indonesia (2013–2023), field and satellite measurements showed a strong negative NDVI–LST correlation (**R² = 0.59**) and a positive built-up-index–LST correlation (**R² = 0.60**), confirming that each point of green cover reduction corresponds quantitatively to measurable surface warming (source not verified).

### 2. Lower Ambient Temperatures Translate into *Lower Heat-Related Mortality*

Several recent studies explicitly bridge the gap from "trees cool air" to "certain people die less":

**London study of UHI-related mortality.** The urban forest in Greater London helped avoid an estimated **153 heat-attributable deaths during 2015–2022** -- approximately **16% of all UHI-related mortality** (source not verified). Areas in the top decile of canopy coverage had average maximum daytime temperatures **0.8 °C lower** and minimum nighttime temperatures **2.0 °C lower** than areas in the bottom decile during heatwaves. The study modelled counterfactual scenarios: increasing tree coverage by 10% (in line with London's existing strategy) would have reduced UHI-related mortality by a further **10%**; maximizing tree coverage would have reduced it by **55%**. By mid-century under RCP8.5, current planting would avoid ~23 additional death/year; maximal coverage would avoid **131 more deaths annually** (source not verified).

**Global mortality burden study.** The same global analysis cited above -- across 11,534 urban areas and 830 locations in 53 countries using mortality data from the Multi-Country Multi-City Collaborative Research Network -- estimated that during 2000–2019, roughly **3.15 million total deaths (2.48% of all deaths)** were attributable to heat exposure under current greenness levels. Increasing EVI by 10%, 20%, and 30% would reduce the *attributable fraction* of heat-related deaths (as a proportion of total deaths) by **0.67, 0.80, and 0.91 percentage points**, respectively, compared to current baselines. Southern Europe was modelled to have the largest relative decrease in attributable heat-mortality fraction **(source not verified)**. Taken together, this study argues that increasing greenness by 30% could avert roughly **2.5–3 million heat-attributable deaths over 2000–2019**.

**China mortality mitigation.** The national-scale Chinese study of 232 cities quantified the avoided-mortality pathway explicitly: heatwaves from 2001–2021 contributed to annual averages of **~23,159 ischemic heart disease deaths, ~39,763 stroke deaths, and ~116,659 COPD deaths**. Projected greenery expansion could prevent approximately **19,764–26,360 annual deaths** under future climate scenarios. Importantly, the protection was disproportionate by age: *elderly populations experienced outsized cardiovascular benefits, while a younger cohort saw reduced COPD mortality* (source not verified).

**Jiangsu Province ecosystem-service analysis.** In an evaluation spanning three cities (Nanjing, Suzhou, Yancheng), cooling service and biodiversity ecosystem services were shown to **reduce heat-related mortality risk**; the strongest protective effects were for cardiorespiratory disease, stroke, and COPD. Women and elderly residents' heat-related mortality risks were *more strongly affected* by green ecosystem services than other demographic groups (source not verified).

### 3. Equity, Demographics, and Effectiveness Heterogeneity

**Environmental inequity.** A PRISMA-aligned systematic review of urban heat impacts (Zheng et al., 2026) documented that disadvantaged populations experience **26–45% higher heat-related mortality risk** and **3–4 °C greater temperature exposure** than affluent communities, even after controlling for income. The review found that *optimal intervention type depends on context*: "urban trees being more effective in disadvantaged areas, while cool roofs are better suited to affluent zones." This pattern likely reflects denser existing canopy (and thus higher marginal cooling) in heat-vulnerable neighborhoods, as well as differing baseline exposure intensities.

**Age and vulnerability.** Multiple reviews converge: the elderly (≥65 years), individuals with cardiopulmonary disease, women, and functionally impaired populations are disproportionately affected by UHI-driven heat mortality. A review synthesizing 221 Chinese-language and 379 English-language articles on plant landscapes' role in older adults' health found that a **10% increase in green space area lowers ambient temperature by 0.2–0.9 °C**, urban trees achieve an average cooling of **2.8 °C**, and tree belts ≥40 m wide reduce PM₂.₅ by up to **67%** -- suggesting *both* thermal and air-purification channels through which vegetation confers heat-related health protection (source not verified).

### 4. Mechanisms and Context-Dependence

**Green roofs.** Provide localized cooling, especially valuable in high-density urban cores where parks and trees are difficult to install. Cooling is more modest and spatially contained than with ground-level green space (Lin & Li, 2025; Zheng et al., 2026).

**Soil moisture as a modulator.** The Belgian citizen-station study is critical here: cooling *breaks down* under extreme heat + low soil moisture + high humidity. This implies that in climate-change-adaptation planning, irrigation of urban green infrastructure is not a luxury -- it is central to maintaining the cooling service (source not verified).

**Nighttime vs. daytime cooling profiles.** Trees provide strong daytime shading; grass and shrub cover provide relatively more nighttime cooling (which is critical for thermoregulatory recovery), but nighttime cooling *also* weakens under humidity and cloud cover (source not verified).

### Bottom Line

The literature converges on a strong conclusion: **urban tree cover and green space are a demonstrably effective nature-based strategy for mitigating both UHI temperatures and heat-related mortality.** The per-person temperature reductions per-unit-green-cover may seem small (a few tenths of a degree), but because the mortality–temperature dose-response curve steepens dramatically above ~27–30 °C, even a 0.15–0.20 °C reduction in ambient air temperature can avert tens of thousands of deaths annually at the city or national scale. However, effectiveness is **strongly context-dependent** -- particularly on soil moisture, plant type (trees > grasses for daytime, grass/shrubs for nighttime), and spatial distribution -- and interventions must be matched to local hydrology, climate, and equity needs.
