## Remote Work & Hybrid Work: What Research Shows on Productivity & Retention

### I. Productivity Findings

#### (1) Positive Productivity Effects in Individual-Task Roles

**Bloom, Liang, Roberts & Ying (2015)** conducted a randomized control trial at cTrip.com involving 250 call center employees (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, Vol. 130, No. 1, pp. 165–218). They found that **call center employees who volunteered to work from home experienced a 13% performance increase** over nine months — 9% from working more minutes (fewer breaks and sick days) and 4% from completing more calls per minute (attributed to a quieter, more convenient environment). Attrition was halved among remote workers, though their promotion rates fell.

**Choudhury, Foroughi & Larson (2021)** studied U.S. Patent and Trademark Office examiners allowed to "work from anywhere," finding that geographic flexibility **increased employee output by 4.4%** with no increase in rework (*Strategic Management Journal*, DOI: 10.1002/smj.3251 — title: "Work-From-Anywhere: The Productivity Effects of Geographic Flexibility"). They documented increased observable effort as a plausible mechanism.

#### (2) Null Productivity Effects in Knowledge-Worker / Collaborative Roles

**Bloom, Han & Liang (2023)** conducted a large RCT at Trip.com (then merged into cTrip) with **1,612 graduate engineers, marketing, and finance employees** over six months. Their key finding on productivity was null: **no statistically significant impact on performance reviews, promotion rates, or lines of code written**. The paper states: "Given the large 1,612 employee size of this experimental sample, this rules out substantial positive or negative impacts of hybrid-WFH on productivity" and that this result "rules out the large negative impacts on performance some detractors have claimed" (NBER Working Paper No. 30292, NBER; also published as *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, Vol. 37, No. 4, Fall 2023, DOI: 10.1257/jep.37.4.23).

A **systematic review (source not verified)** analyzing 26 studies from 112 candidates concluded that the impact of WFH on productivity depends heavily on work nature and employer characteristics, **"with a majority reporting a positive impact and few documenting no difference or a negative impact."**

#### (3) The Innovation / Collaboration Concern

A key gap in the evidence: Bloom et al. noted that prior experiments focused on individual tasks (call centers, patent examiners), not collaborative creative work. Their Trip.com null result was notable precisely because it involved **graduate employees working in teams and creating new products**, suggesting hybrid WFH has no detectable negative impact even in less individually-focused roles — though the authors acknowledge innovation measurement is harder than output-volume measurement.

### II. Employee Retention Findings (Strongest Consensus Area)

The most consistent finding across studies is that remote/hybrid work **substantially reduces employee attrition**.
